2. A common-face test is also completely explicit: write each tetrahedron in barycentric halfspaces,
intersect the two finite lists of halfspaces, and enumerate the possible vertices by solving
triples of bounding equations. The intersection is a common face precisely when all its vertices
are common tetrahedron vertices. Since the candidate tetrahedra lie in Pn, pairwise face
intersections and the volume equality imply that they cover the slice. Otherwise their finite
closed union would omit a nonempty relatively open set, which has positive volume. The
boundary tests make the translates agree on every shared face. This proves that every passing
collection gives the claimed invariant fan.
3. Conversely, intersect any invariant smooth refinement with Pn. A smooth maximal cone has
four primitive generators with determinant ±1, and its slice is one of the stated tetrahedra.
Finitely many cone orbits give finitely many rays in this prism, hence some maximum height
H. No additional bottom rays are permitted by the boundary condition; every other ray has
the form (21.1). The resulting collection passes all three tests. Thus the search at each height
is finite, and the procedure is exhaustive.
4. Properness of the quotient forces the finite-orbit condition. Each full-dimensional cone has a
closed torus orbit consisting of one point with q = r = 0. These points form a closed discrete
set: an affine chart of a full-dimensional cone contains exactly its own closed torus orbit. After
the properly discontinuous period quotient the points are indexed by cone orbits and remain
closed and discrete. Infinitely many such orbits would therefore give an infinite closed discrete
subset of a compact central fibre, which is impossible.
