5. Make this complex strict by barycentric star subdivisions, in decreasing cone dimension. At
the moment a stratum is blown up, all of its deeper self-intersections have already been
separated. Its closure is therefore smooth, and different translates in the chosen orbit are
disjoint. The star at the sum of the primitive basis generators is the ordinary blow-up of that
closure. Repeating over the finitely many cone orbits gives smooth strict toroidal embeddings.
This is the geometric barycentric subdivision used in (Abramovich et al., 2002, Section 5.6,
pp.565–566); its analytic realization by the same monomial ideals is described in (Abramovich
et al., 2002, Section 5.5, pp. 564–565). Pure q-strata are included in this preparatory step
and in the comparison of the punctured r-families.
6. Intersect the two subdivided fans, and take a regular subdivision, to obtain their explicit
common strict toroidal modification. Apply toroidal weak factorization to each of its two
subdivision maps. The needed theorem is the finite polyhedral-complex factorization by
nonsingular star subdivisions and inverse subdivisions, (Abramovich et al., 2002, Theorem 1.6.1,
p. 543; Theorem 2.7.1, pp. 551–552). The corrected form for a morphism states that its
centres are smooth irreducible closed strata and that all intermediate complexes map to the
target (Matsuki, 2000, Section 2, p.2).
7. The complexes are finite, so the cited combinatorial factorization applies. The analytic
subdivision–modification correspondence of (Abramovich et al., 2002, Section 5.5, pp. 564–565)
realizes each of its finitely many stars by the indicated monomial blow-up. The correspondence
respects the face identifications and the bundle units, so the local analytic blow-ups glue.
All intermediate morphisms are proper over the target of the subdivision map, hence over a
sufficiently small r-disc. They remain smooth, and both parameters remain monomials times
units. This gives the required finite chain of analytic blow-ups and blow-downs between the
total neighbourhoods.
8. We classify the connected centres in this chain. A nontrivial centre has codimension at least
two. For a cone having a positive q-height ray, its period stabilizer is trivial: a power of a
stabilizing element fixes each of its finitely many primitive rays; the positive q-height first
forces the exponent of G1 to vanish and then the exponent of G2 to vanish. The same argument
holds for pure bottom cones. The normalization of the closure of their torus stratum is the
toric variety of its star. If the closed stratum is smooth, this finite birational normalization
map is an isomorphism.
9. For a mixed cone the centre is contained in q = r = 0; its star is complete, and it is a compact
smooth toric point, curve, or surface. It has free integral cohomology in even degrees only.
For a pure top centre, the cone must have dimension two. Its stabilizer is precisely ⟨G1⟩,
and the chart quotient and the plumbing identification show that its dense open part is the
corresponding open part of S. The compact smooth centre is therefore a surface birational
to the rational surface S. Equivalently, its proper map to the unmodified top stratum is a
birational map of smooth surfaces. It is a smooth rational surface and again has free even
cohomology. It need not remain isomorphic to S after earlier mixed modifications.
10. For a pure bottom centre the cone has dimension two or three. Its star gives a smooth toric
surface or curve proper over the r-disc. The induced character r is primitive on its quotient
lattice, since it was primitive before quotienting by a smooth cone on which it vanishes. Its
generic fibre is therefore connected, and is respectively a complete toric P1 or a point. In the
surface case the ordered two-dimensional fan gives a chain of rational curves as the central
support, possibly with positive multiplicities. In the curve case the support is one point.
Monomial gradient retraction, as in 21.16, shows that the ordinary integral cohomology of the
centre is free and even, with H0 = Z and H4 = 0. These centres are not compact; this is why
they must be distinguished from the first two types.
