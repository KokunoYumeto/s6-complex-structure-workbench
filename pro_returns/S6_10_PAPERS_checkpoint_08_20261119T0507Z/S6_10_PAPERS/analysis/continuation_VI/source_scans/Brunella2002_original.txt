    1. Basic definitions


    Let X be a smooth complex surface (the smoothness assumption will be soon relaxed).
A foliation F on X is given by an open covering {Uj } of X and holomorphic vector fields
vj ∈ H 0 (Uj , ΘX ) with isolated zeroes such that

                                 vi = gij vj    on     Ui ∩ Uj

for some nonvanishing holomorphic functions gij ∈ H 0 (Ui ∩ Uj , OX   ∗
                                                                        ). This allows to glue
together the local orbits of the vector fields {vj } to obtain the leaves of F . The singular
set Sing(F ) of F is the discrete subset of X defined by Sing(F ) ∩ Uj = { zeroes of vj }.
    The functions {gij } form a multiplicative cocycle and define a holomorphic line bundle
KF , called canonical bundle of F . The relations vi = gij vj allow to construct a global
holomorphic section s ∈ H 0 (X, KF ⊗ ΘX ), vanishing only on Sing(F ). If TF denotes the
dual of KF (the tangent bundle of F ) we therefore have an exact sequence of sheaves
induced by s
                             0 −→ TF −→ ΘX −→ IZ · NF −→ 0

for a suitable line bundle NF (the normal bundle of F ) and a suitable ideal sheaf IZ
supported on Sing(F ) (see e.g. [Fri, Chapter 2]).
    In a dual way, the foliation F can be also defined by (kernels of) holomorphic 1-forms
with isolated zeroes ωj ∈ H 0 (Uj , Ω1X ) satisfying

                                ωi = fij ωj      on    Ui ∩ Uj

for a suitable cocycle {fij }. This cocycle actually defines the normal bundle NF , and if
NF∗ denotes its dual (the conormal bundle of F ) we obtain an exact sequence of sheaves

                            0 −→ NF∗ −→ Ω1X −→ IZ · KF −→ 0

which is the dual of the previous one.
    The canonical and the conormal bundle of F are related by the formula

                                        KX = KF ⊗ NF∗

where KX (= Ω2X ) is the canonical bundle of the surface X. Indeed, the choice of a local
nonvanishing holomorphic 2-form induces, by contraction, a local isomorphism between
vector fields and 1-forms generating F .

    Let us consider now the case of a singular surface X. In fact, we shall need only the
case where X has only cyclic quotient singularities, i.e. around each p ∈ Sing(X) the
surface is of the type B2 /Γk,h where B2 is the unit ball in C2 and Γk,h is the cyclic group
                                      2πi    2πi
of order k generated by (z, w) 7→ (e k z, e k h w), for suitable coprime positive integers
k, h with 0 < h < k. Equivalently [BPV, pages 80-85], such a singularity arises from the
contraction of a Hirzebruch–Jung string on a smooth surface (a chain of rational curves,
each one of selfintersection ≤ −2).
     Because cyclic quotient singularities are normal, a foliation F on such a surface can
be simply defined as a foliation on X \ Sing(X). If p ∈ Sing(X) and U ≃ B2 /Γ is a
neighbourhood of p, then F |U\{p} can be lifted to B2 \ {0}, and on that covering the
foliation can be defined by a holomorphic vector field which holomorphically extends to
0. We shall say that F is nonsingular at p if that extension is nonvanishing at 0. In
other words, if F is not singular at p then on U ≃ B2 /Γ the foliation is the quotient of
the vertical or horizontal foliation on B2 (up to an equivariant biholomorphism). Even
if many things can be done in greater generality, we shall always assume that F is not
singular at singular points of X:


                                 Sing(F ) ∩ Sing(X) = ∅.


     In this context, leaves of F are defined as in the smooth case, by glueing together
local leaves through nonsingular points of F . The only difference is that if p ∈ Sing(X)
has order k then the local leaf of F through p is an orbifold in which p is affected by the
multiplicity k. Indeed, this local leaf on B2 /Γ is the quotient of a disc D ⊂ B2 by a
k-cyclic group. Thus, leaves of F are orbifolds injectively immersed (in orbifold’s sense) in
X \ Sing(F ), and giving a partition of X \ Sing(F ) into disjoint subsets.
    Given a foliation F on a surface X with (at most) cyclic quotient singularities, we
can still define its canonical sheaf KF , for instance by taking the direct image under the
inclusion X \ Sing(X) → X. However, it is no more a genuine line bundle (an element
of P ic(X)) but only a Q-bundle (an element of P ic(X) ⊗ Q). Indeed, if p ∈ Sing(X)
                                                                ⊗k
has order k then KF is not locally free at p, but its k-power KF    is, and moreover k is
                                                                   ∂
the minimal positive integer with that property (the vector field ∂w on B2 is not Γk,h -
                            ∂ ⊗k
invariant, but its power ( ∂w ) is). Anyway, for many things Q-bundles are as good as
line bundles.
    Similar considerations hold also for TF , NF , NF∗ , and of course KX . We still have the
equality KX = KF ⊗ NF∗ , as sheaves or Q-bundles.

    2. Basic formulae


    We shall need many times some elementary formulae which compute the degree of
KF over compact curves in X.
   As before, let X be a surface with at most cyclic quotient singularities, and let F be a
foliation on X, with Sing(F )∩Sing(X) = ∅. Let C ⊂ X be a compact connected (possibly
singular) curve, and suppose that each irreducible component of C is not invariant by F .
For every p ∈ C we can define an index tang(F , C, p) which measure the tangency order
of F with C at p (and thus which is 0 for a generic p ∈ C, where we have transversality).
    If p 6∈ Sing(X) the definition is the following: we take a local equation f of C at p, a
local holomorphic vector field v generating F around p, and we set

                                                         Op
                             tang(F , C, p) = dimC
                                                     < f, v(f ) >

where Op is the local algebra of X at p (germs of holomorphic functions), v(f ) is the Lie
derivative of f along v, < f, v(f ) > is the ideal in Op generated by f and v(f ). This index
is finite, because C is not F -invariant; it is a nonnegative integer; it is 0 iff p 6∈ Sing(F )
and F is transverse to C at p.
    If p ∈ Sing(X), we take a neighbourhood U ≃ B2 /Γ and we lift F |U and C ∩ U on
B2 . We compute the index on B2 (at 0) and we divide the result by k, the order of the
singularity. This is, by definition, tang(F , C, p). It is a nonnegative rational number, and
it is 0 iff F is transverse to C at p (in the sense that the lift of F on B2 is transverse to
the lift of C at 0).
     We also have at our disposal the selfintersection C · C of C in X: it is the degree on C
of the Q-bundle OX (C), and it is a rational number, possibly a noninteger one if C passes
through Sing(X) (see e.g. [Sak] for the intersection theory on normal surfaces). We then
have the following formula for the degree of the Q-bundle KF on C:

                               KF · C = −C · C + tang(F , C)
                      P
where tang(F , C) =      p∈C tang(F , C, p) (a finite sum).   This is proved in [Br1, page 23]
when X is smooth, but the general case can be handled along the same lines. The most
important consequence of this formula is the inequality

                                      (KF + C) · C ≥ 0

where the equality is realized if and only if F is everywhere transverse to C.

    3. Singularities



    In order to simplify some statements and some proofs, and also to get a more coherent
theory, we will need some assumptions on the singularities of the foliation [Br1, Chapter
1] [C-S] [M-M] [MR1] [MR2].
    A singularity p ∈ Sing(F ) is called reduced (in Seidenberg’s sense) if F around p
is generated by a vector field v whose linear part (Dv)p has eigenvalues λ1 , λ2 such that
either λ1 6= 0 6= λ2 and λ1 /λ2 6∈ Q+ , or λ1 6= 0 = λ2 . In the former case p is called
nondegenerate, in the latter case a saddle-node.
    A fundamental result of Seidenberg [M-M] [C-S] [Br1, page 13] says that, given any

reduced foliations, i.e. foliations all of whose singularities are reduced.
    Note also that the class of reduced singularities is stable by blow-ups: the blow-up
of a reduced singularity produces, on the exceptional divisor, only reduced singularities.
This remains true even if we blow up a regular point p ∈ X \ {Sing(F ) ∪ Sing(X)}, or if
we take the resolution of a point p ∈ Sing(X).

singularities.

diffeomorphism of period equal to the order k of the singularity. This is consistent with
the fact that the local fundamental group of the orbifold Lp at p is the cyclic group of
order k.




    4. Nef models


     As the title suggests, one of the purposes of [MQ1] is to develop a “Mori theory” for
foliations on algebraic surfaces (or even on higher dimensional varieties). Therefore, as in
the ordinary Mori theory (see e.g. [M-P]), one of the first steps should be the construction
of birational models of foliations whose canonical bundles have suitable semipositivity
properties.
    In what follows, X will denote a complex projective surface with (at most) cyclic
quotient singularities. Recall that a Q-bundle L (or a Q-divisor D) on X is called pseu-
doeffective if L · H ≥ 0 for every ample divisor H on X. This is equivalent to say that
L · C ≥ 0 for every irreducible curve C ⊂ X whose selfintersection C · C is nonnegative.
If the same inequality holds for every irreducible curve C, regardless its selfintersection,
then L is said to be nef (numerically eventually free). A useful result of Zariski and Fujita
[Fuj] [Sak] states that any pseudoeffective Q-bundle L has a Zariski decomposition: it can
be uniquely written as
                                         L=P +N
where P (the positive part) is a nef Q-bundle and N (the negative part) is an effective
                                                                                Pn
Q-bundle whose support is contractible and orthogonal to P (i.e. N = OX ( j=1 aj Cj ),
with aj ∈ Q+ , Cj irreducible curves with (Ci · Cj )i,j negative definite, and P · Cj = 0 for
every j).
    Let now F be a foliation on X, with as usual Sing(F ) ∩ Sing(X) = ∅, and let KF ∈
P ic(X) ⊗Q be its canonical bundle. The following result has been proved (in a much more
general context) by Miyaoka [Miy] and Shepherd-Barron [ShB] using positive characteristic

                                             8
arguments (see also [M-P, Lecture III]). We shall reproduce here a characteristic zero proof
due to Bogomolov and McQuillan [B-M], which reduces the statement to a classical result
of Arakelov on fibrations by curves [Ara] [Szp] [BPV, pages 107-110].
     Theorem 1 [Miy] [ShB] [B-M]. Let X be a complex projective surface with at most
cyclic quotient singularities, and let F be a foliation on X with reduced singularities. Then
the following two statements are equivalent:
i) F is a rational fibration, i.e. a fibration whose generic fibre is a rational curve CP 1 ;
ii) KF is not pseudoeffective.
    Proof.
    i) ⇒ ii). This is obvious: a generic fibre C satisfies C · C = 0 and KF · C = −χ(C) =
−2 < 0, thus KF cannot be pseudoeffective.
    ii) ⇒ i). If KF is not pseudoeffective then we can find an irreducible curve C ⊂ X
with C · C > 0 and KF · C < 0. We may also suppose that C is smooth, disjoint from
Sing(F ) ∪ Sing(X), and that it is not F -invariant (C is the zero set of a generic section
of a very ample bundle).
    On the projective threefold Y = X × C let us consider the 1-dimensional foliation G
which is tangent to each stratum X × {c}, c ∈ C, and which coincides there with F . Let
D ⊂ Y be the diagonal curve {(c, c)|c ∈ C} ⊂ X × C. On a neighbourhood U of D we
can construct a smooth complex surface Z ⊂ U ⊂ Y by glueing together the local leaves
of G through points of D: note that at each (c, c) ∈ D the foliation G is nonsingular, and
its local leaf through (c, c) is a disk not tangent to D (even if the local leaf of F through
c may be tangent to C).
    This surface Z contains D, and we can compute the selfintersection of D in Z. Because
G is tangent to Z and everywhere transverse to D in Z, we have (D · D)Z = TG · D. But
TG is the pull-back of TF by the projection Y → X, and D projects to C under the same
projection, thus we have
                                  (D · D)Z = −KF · C > 0

and in particular Z is a so-called pseudoconcave surface [And]. By a theorem of Andreotti
[And] (which, in this special case, is reproved in [B-M] by an easier formal argument) the
Zariski-closure of Z in Y has the same dimension as Z: there exists an algebraic surface
W ⊂ Y which contains Z.
                                                                           π
     Such a surface W is G-invariant (for Z is), and the projection W → C to the second
factor of X × C is a fibration which coincides with G|W (for this is true on Z). The curve
D ⊂ W is a section of this fibration, and the positivity of its selfintersection implies, by
Arakelov’s theorem [Ara] [Szp], that π is a rational fibration. By projecting from W to

implies that C is F -invariant: otherwise we should have (KF + C) · C ≥ 0, which is not
the case. We can therefore apply the formula

                                KF · C = −χorb (C) + Z(F , C).

From KF · C < 0 we obtain χorb (C) > Z(F , C) ≥ 0 (C has at most normal crossing
singularities, because F is reduced). In particular, C is a smooth rational curve, possibly
passing through Sing(X). Observe now that C must intersect Sing(F ), otherwise by
Camacho–Sad formula we would deduce C · C = 0. Hence Z(F , C) ≥ 1, and consequently
χorb (C) > 1. On the other side, χorb (C) ≤ 2 and so Z(F , C) < 2. This leaves open only
the following possibility: C is a smooth rational curve which contains at most 1 point q
from Sing(X) (if q has order k then χorb (C) = 1+ k1 ), and exactly 1 point p from Sing(F ),
with Z(F , C, p) = 1.

                                                10
     In other words, by contracting a curve over which the canonical bundle of the folia-
tion has negative degree we obtain a new surface and a new foliation which still satisfy
our standing assumptions on Sing(X) and Sing(F ). By iterating this procedure a finite
number of steps we finally obtain the following result of McQuillan [MQ1] [Br1].
    Corollary 1. Let X be a complex projective surface with at most cyclic quotient
singularities, and let F be a foliation on X with reduced singularities. Suppose that F is
not a rational fibration. Then there exists a birational morphism (X, F ) → (X ′ , F ′ ) such
that:
i) X ′ is still projective with at most cyclic quotient singularities, and F ′ is still reduced;
ii) the canonical bundle of F ′ is nef.
     It is here that the presence of cyclic quotient singularities becomes unavoidable: even
if X is smooth, it may happen that X ′ is singular. Of course, the contraction X → X ′ is
nothing but the contraction of the support of the negative part of the Zariski decomposition
of KF [MQ1, §III.2] [Br1, page 113], thus the Corollary can be seen as a description of
that negative part.
     We shall say that a foliation F is a nef foliation if KF is nef, so that the previous
results (Seidenberg’s resolution, Miyaoka’s theorem, McQuillan’s contraction) say that
any foliation, which is not birational to a rational fibration, has a birational model which
is reduced and nef. Such a model, in general, is not unique: for instance, the blow-up of
a reduced nef foliation at a singular point is still reduced and nef (whereas nefness is lost
if we blow up a regular point). In order to get uniqueness we need, at least, to contract
more curves, and to obtain the so-called minimal models; but even after these additional
contractions uniqueness may fail, and we refer to [MQ1] [Br1] [Br3] for a more complete
discussion of this point and related ones.
    From now on, we shall concentrate our attention to reduced nef foliations. Given such
a foliation F , with canonical bundle KF ∈ P ic(X)⊗Q, we define the numerical Kodaira
dimension ν(F ) of F as the numerical Kodaira dimension of KF , that is:
• ν(F ) = 2 if KF · KF > 0;
• ν(F ) = 1 if KF · KF = 0 but KF is not numerically trivial (there exists C such that
KF · C > 0);
• ν(F ) = 0 if KF is numerically trivial (KF · C = 0 for every C).
(Because KF is nef, we have KF · KF ≥ 0).
    If ν(F ) = 2 then F is said to be of general type. In some sense, most foliations
belong to this class. Our ultimate task will be the classification of foliations which are not
of general type.

    5. Numerically trivial foliations


    A reduced nef foliation F on a projective surface X is said to be numerically trivial
if ν(F ) = 0, that is its canonical bundle KF has zero degree on every curve C ⊂ X. The
classification of these foliations is rather easy: not surprisingly, they are related to folia-
tions whose canonical bundle is holomorphically trivial, i.e. foliations which are globally
generated by a single global holomorphic vector field with isolated zeroes.

unpleasant.

    7. Riccati and Turbulent foliations


    A foliation F on X is a Riccati foliation, resp. a Turbulent foliation, if there
exists a fibration π : X → B whose generic fibre is rational, resp. elliptic, and transverse
to F . Riccati and Turbulent foliations constitute a basic piece of the classification of
nongeneral type foliations, as the next result shows.
    Theorem 4 [MQ1] [Men]. Let F be a reduced nef foliation with
                                      ν(F ) = kod(F ) = 1
on a projective surface X. Then one of the following situations occurs:
i) F is a Riccati foliation;
ii) F is a Turbulent foliation;
iii) F is a nonisotrivial elliptic fibration;
iv) F is an isotrivial fibration of genus ≥ 2.
    Proof.
    Let π : X → B be the Iitaka fibration [M-P] associated to KF . A generic fibre C of
π is a curve over which KF has zero degree. If F coincides with the Iitaka fibration then
0 = KF · C = −χ(C), thus C is elliptic. The nonisotriviality of π follows from Arakelov’s
theorem [Ara] [Ser], and we are in case iii).
    If F does not coincides with the Iitaka fibration, then 0 = KF ·C = −C 2 +tang(F , C) =
tang(F , C), thus C is transverse to F . If C is rational, resp. elliptic, then F is Riccati,
resp. Turbulent.
     Suppose now that C has genus ≥ 2. The transversality of F to the generic fibres of
π shows that the fibration π is isotrivial, and so it is a locally trivial fibre bundle up to
ramified coverings and birational maps [BPV, Chapter III]. Standard hyperbolic arguments
(finiteness of Aut(fibre), Picard’s theorem) imply that F , after those ramified coverings
and birational maps, becomes a second fibration everywhere transverse to π. In other
words, the universal covering of the surface is D × Σ, with Σ = D or C or CP 1 , and on
that universal covering the foliation is the vertical one, with leaves {∗} × Σ. It follows
easily that F itself is an isotrivial fibration, the isotriviality being given by the fibres of π.
Finally, KF has strictly positive degree on a fibre of F , because that fibre is transverse to
the Iitaka fibration of KF . Thus F has genus ≥ 2, and we are in case iv).

    8. Poincaré metric



     In this section we introduce some analytic tools which will be used to complete the
classification of nongeneral type foliations.
     Let F be a reduced nef foliation on X. Recall that each leaf of F is an orbifold,
injectively immersed in X \ Sing(F ). The universal covering of such a leaf has no multiple

                                                21
point (indeed, this is true already for the holonomy covering), hence it is isomorphic to D

leaf is uniformised by C (parabolic leaf).

counterpart to Theorem 1. We refer to [Dem] for the basic theory of singular hermitian
metrics.

whose curvature is a closed positive current.

    9. Hilbert modular foliations


    With a negligible abuse of terminology, we shall say that a complex projective surface
X (with cyclic quotient singularities) is a Hilbert modular surface [BPV, page 177] if
there exists a (possibly empty) curve C ⊂ X \ Sing(X) such that:
i) each connected component of C is a cycle of smooth rational curves, contractible to a
normal singularity (a “cusp”);
ii) X \ C is uniformised (in orbifold’s sense) by the bidisc D × D, more precisely X =
D × D/Γ where Γ is a discrete subgroup of Aut0 (D × D) = P SL(2, R) × P SL(2, R);
iii) Γ is irreducible, in the sense that it does not contain a finite index subgroup of the
form Γ1 × Γ2 , with Γj ⊂ P SL(2, R), j = 1, 2.
    Such a surface is naturally equipped with two natural foliations F and G, arising
from the horizontal and the vertical foliations on D × D, preserved by Γ. These are
called Hilbert modular foliations. They are both tangent to C, and they are singular
only in correspondence of the normal crossings of C; these singularities are reduced and
nondegenerate (eigenvalues can by computed by Camacho–Sad formula).
    We have KF = NG∗ ⊗ OX (C), the logarithmic conormal bundle of G: indeed, C is
the tangency locus between F and G, and along C the two foliations have a first order
tangency. The results of section 4 show that KF is nef. By the irreducibility hypothesis on
Γ the foliations are not fibrations, therefore by the logarithmic Castelnuovo–De Franchis–
Bogomolov lemma [Br1, Chapter 6] we have kod(F ) = kod(NG∗ ⊗ OX (C)) ≤ 0. The case
kod(F ) = 0 is however excluded because F is not a parabolic foliation, so we finally obtain:
F is a reduced nef foliation with ν(F ) = 1 and kod(F ) = −∞. The same of course holds
for G.
     The following result completes the classification of nongeneral type foliations.
     Theorem 7 [Br2] [MQ1]. Let F be a reduced nef foliation with

    10. Kähler surfaces


    Up to now we have mostly considered the case of foliations on projective surfaces.
Let us explain in this last section how to extend the previous results to the slightly more
general case of compact Kähler surfaces. More precisely, we shall draw a complete list of
all foliations on compact Kähler nonprojective surfaces: this is consistent with the fact
that on such a surface a foliation cannot be of general type, because a line bundle cannot
have Kodaira dimension 2.
    Instead of working with a special model of the foliation (reduced, nef), it is simpler
here to work with a special model of the surface. Thus, let X be a minimal smooth
compact Kähler nonprojective surface. According to Kodaira classification [BPV], the
algebraic dimension a(X) is either 1, in which case X has a unique elliptic fibration, or 0,
in which case X is a torus or a K3 surface. Let F be any foliation on X. We distinguish
three cases:

                                               28
    a(X) = 1. Let π : X → B be the elliptic fibration. Any compact curve in X is
contained in some fibre of π. In particular, if F is different from the fibration then the
tangency locus between F and π is contained in some fibres. In other words, F is transverse
to a generic fibre of π, and so F is a Turbulent foliation.
    X is a torus with a(X) = 0. We may take on X = C2 /G a foliation G generated by
a constant vector field and tangent to F at some point. The tangency locus between F
and G is not empty and it cannot be a curve, because such an X contains no curve. Thus
it remains only the possibility F = G, i.e. F is a Kronecker foliation, generated by a
constant vector field.
       X is a K3 surface with a(X) = 0. Such a surface admits, by Yau’s theorem [BPV,
page 40], a Kähler–Einstein metric in each Kähler cohomology class, which implies the
semistability of its tangent bundle [Fri, Chapter 4]. That is, if ω ∈ A1,1 (X) is any Kähler
form on X, then we have the semistability inequality

                                                  1
                               c1 (TF ) · [ω] ≤     c1 (X) · [ω] = 0
                                                  2

i.e.
                                       c1 (KF ) · [ω] ≥ 0.

This inequality plays here the same rôle as Theorem 1 in the projective case: it says that
KF is pseudoeffective [Dem] [Lam]. Looking at the proof of [Fuj], one realizes that such a
line bundle has a Zariski decomposition: as a Q-bundle, it can be written as

                                         KF = P + N

where N is an effective Q-bundle with contractible support and P is a Q-bundle which
satisfies: i) P ·[ω] ≥ 0 for every Kähler form ω; ii) P ·C ≥ 0 for every compact curve C ⊂ X;
iii) P · D = 0 for every compact curve D ⊂ Supp(N ). From i) and ii) it follows [Lam] that
P · P ≥ 0. But on a K3 surface with zero algebraic dimension there exists only one line
bundle L with L · L ≥ 0: the trivial one (by Riemann–Roch we have h0 (L) + h0 (L∗ ) ≥ 2
and by a(X) = 0 we have h0 (L) ≤ 1 and h0 (L∗ ) ≤ 1). It follows that P is numerically
                            ⊗n
trivial, or more precisely KF   = OX (E) for some n > 0 and some effective divisor E
