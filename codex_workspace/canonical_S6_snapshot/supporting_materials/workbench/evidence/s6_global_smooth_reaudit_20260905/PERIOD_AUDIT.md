## 2. Retained objects, coordinates, parameters, and laws

The base is `B ≅ P^1`, with three marked points `p_0,p_1,p_2` and the actual affine coordinate `t` satisfying

\[
t(p_1)=0,\qquad t(p_2)=1,\qquad t(p_0)=\infty.
\]

The orbifold uniformization is

\[
\pi:\mathfrak h_z\longrightarrow B\setminus\{p_0\},
\qquad
\Delta=\langle g_1,g_2\mid g_1^3=g_2^4=1\rangle,
\qquad g_0=(g_1g_2)^{-1}.
\]

Here `h_z` is the simply connected hyperbolic domain with the specified holomorphic triangle-group action. The chosen lifts `z_j` have stabilizers generated by `g_j`; the retained derivatives are

\[
\lambda_1=g_1'(z_1)=e^{-2\pi i/3},\qquad
\lambda_2=g_2'(z_2)=-i.
\]

The Cayley coordinates `s_j` obey `s_j(g_j z)=lambda_j s_j(z)`. Thus the local coarse coordinates are `s_1^3,s_2^4`, up to holomorphic units. The simultaneous derivative choice is geometrically available: for a hyperbolic triangle, the successive reflection products `x,y,z` have orders `3,4,infinity` and `xyz=1`; putting `g_1=x^{-1},g_2=y^{-1}` gives both negative rotations and `g_1g_2=g_1 z g_1^{-1}`, hence a parabolic product. No independent reversal of just one elliptic generator is made.

We retain the functions and their actual codomains

\[
\tau:\mathfrak h_z\to\mathfrak h=\{w\in\mathbb C:\Im w>0\},
\qquad \mu,\beta:\mathfrak h_z\to\mathbb C,
\]

and all the original constants:

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
&\tau&\mu&\beta\\ \hline
g_1& (\tau-1)/\tau&(1-\mu)/\tau&\beta+2-6(1-\mu)^2/\tau\\
g_2&-1/\tau&1+\mu/\tau&\beta-3-6\mu^2/\tau\\
g_0&\tau-1&\mu&\beta+1.
\end{array}
\tag{A1}
\]

The retained modular equation and elliptic values are

\[
j(\tau)=1728(t\circ\pi),\qquad
\tau(z_1)=\rho=e^{\pi i/3},\qquad \tau(z_2)=i.
\tag{A2}
\]

The cusp condition is imposed on the one component `U_0` stabilized by `<g_0>`: the invariant functions `mu` and `b=beta+tau` must extend holomorphically in the actual cusp coordinate `t_c`. This is equivalent to the original boundedness conditions after shrinking its radius, by the removable-singularity theorem in one variable. It is not a boundedness condition on every translate of `U_0`.

The marked lattice is

\[
\Lambda=\mathbb Z\langle\widehat\gamma,\widehat u,\widehat w,\widehat\delta\rangle,
\qquad
\Pi=[Z\mid I_2],\qquad
Z=\begin{pmatrix}6\mu&\tau\\\beta&\mu\end{pmatrix},
\qquad D=\Im\beta-\frac{6(\Im\mu)^2}{\Im\tau}.
\tag{A3}
\]

Every entry of (A1)–(A3), including `1728`, `6`, `+2`, `-3`, the inverse cusp word, the ordered lattice, and the two derivative signs, agrees with the inspected original Definition 3.1 and Definition 3.3.

## 3. Global existence and identification of tau

For clarity about constants in the independent modular source, its Eisenstein functions are `G_k(w)=sum'_(m,n)(mw+n)^(-2k)`. The present normalized functions are

\[
E_4=G_2/(2\zeta(4)),\qquad E_6=G_3/(2\zeta(6)),
\qquad \Delta_{\rm mod}=\frac{E_4^3-E_6^2}{1728}.
\]

Milne's discriminant is `(2pi)^12 Delta_mod`. Thus his displayed formulas yield exactly

\[
j=E_4^3/\Delta_{\rm mod},\qquad
E_4=1+O(q),\quad E_6=1+O(q),\quad
\Delta_{\rm mod}=q(1+O(q)),\quad j=q^{-1}+744+O(q).
\tag{A4}
\]

The modular quotient is `h/PSL_2(Z) ≅ C`; only the orbits of `rho` and `i` have nontrivial stabilizers, of orders three and two. Therefore deleting their orbits makes `j` a regular unramified covering over `C\{0,1728}`. Its local degrees at those orbits are three and two. The zeros of `E_4,E_6` are simple at their respective elliptic orbits, and `Delta_mod` is nonzero in `h`.

Put `J_B=1728 t pi`. Delete its fibers over `0,1728` from `h_z`. Every loop in the resulting punctured simply connected surface is a finite product of conjugates of small meridians: fill it in `h_z`, perturb the filling away from the locally finite deleted set except at finitely many transverse points, and cut the filling along paths from those points to its boundary. A meridian about a point over `p_1` maps to the third power of an order-three modular monodromy. A meridian over `p_2` maps to the fourth power of an order-two modular monodromy. Both are trivial. The covering lifting criterion therefore gives a global holomorphic lift `tau` on the punctured domain with `j tau=J_B`.

At every deleted point, a biholomorphism `chi:h→D` makes `chi tau` bounded. Its holomorphic extension cannot attain modulus one: the maximum principle would make it constant of modulus one on the disc, contradicting its punctured values. Hence `tau` extends into `h`, not merely into its closure. This constructs `tau:h_z→h`.

The deck property gives a homomorphism `phi:Delta→PSL_2(Z)` characterized by `tau(gz)=phi(g)tau(z)`. Specifically, the two lifts differ by one deck transformation on the connected punctured domain; equality extends across its deleted points. Substitution gives `phi(gh)=phi(g)phi(h)` because the action convention is `(gh)z=g(hz)`.

Send the first elliptic value to the prescribed `rho` by one modular deck transformation. The local order identity `3 ord_(z_1)(tau-rho)=3` shows `tau-rho` has order one. Differentiating equivariance gives `phi(g_1)'(rho)=lambda_1`, so

\[
\phi(g_1)=S_1,\qquad S_1(w)=(w-1)/w.
\]

At `z_2`, after locating its image in the modular orbit of `i`, the local identity `2 ord_(z_2)(gamma^{-1}tau-i)=4` gives order two. Therefore `phi(g_2)` is not identity, since invariance under `s_2↦-i s_2` would force that order to be divisible by four. Its order is exactly two.

Let `P=phi(g_0)`. A sufficiently small cusp component `U_0` maps into one component `K` of `j^{-1}({|w|>R})`. As `g_0 U_0=U_0`, the nonempty subsets `tau(U_0)` and `P tau(U_0)` coincide; consequently `P K=K`. The modular cusp components are the modular translates of a neighborhood of infinity; their boundary cusp is rational. Its stabilizer consists of the identity and parabolic translations in an integral cusp basis. The identity case is impossible here: the relation `S_1 phi(g_2)P=1` would make an element of order two equal `S_1^{-1}`, of order three. Thus `P` is a nontrivial integral parabolic.

Choose its trace `+2` lift. If `(p,r)^t` is its primitive integral fixed vector, completion to an oriented integral basis gives

\[
\widetilde P=I+k\begin{pmatrix}-pr&p^2\\-r^2&pr\end{pmatrix},
\quad \gcd(p,r)=1,\quad k\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}.
\]

With `S1tilde=[[1,-1],[1,0]]`, direct multiplication gives

\[
\operatorname{tr}(\widetilde S_1^{-1}\widetilde P^{-1})
=1+k(p^2-pr+r^2).
\]

The corresponding projective transformation is `phi(g_2)`, so its trace is zero. Since `p^2-pr+r^2=(p-r/2)^2+3r^2/4` is a positive integer, this forces `k=-1` and `p^2-pr+r^2=1`. Modulo simultaneous sign, its vectors are `(1,0),(1,1),(0,1)`. Multiplication by `S1tilde` cycles these three classes. Conjugating the entire deck identification by the corresponding power of `S_1` preserves both `rho` and the prescribed `S_1` and sends `P` to `w↦w-1`. The exact group relation then gives `phi(g_2)(w)=-1/w`, whose fixed point in `h` is `i`. Thus every part of (A1)–(A2) for `tau` holds with the retained constants and signs. This is a change in the initial lift identifying the demanded functions, not an alteration of the original base or action.

Uniqueness follows as well. Two such lifts differ by a modular deck map commuting with both `S_1` and `S`; it fixes their distinct interior fixed points `rho` and `i`. An automorphism of the disc with two distinct interior fixed points is identity: conjugate the first to zero, use Schwarz's lemma to identify the automorphism as a rotation, and then use the second fixed point. Thus the two lifts coincide.

At the distinguished cusp `q=e^{2pi i tau}` is invariant. The local inverse of `j` near its simple pole gives `q=t_c u_1(t_c)`, with `u_1` a holomorphic unit. No leading constant is discarded: if the actual coordinate has

\[
t=\kappa t_c^{-1}+O(1),\qquad \kappa\ne0,
\]

then (A4) gives `u_1(0)=1/(1728 kappa)`. Hence, with `h_c=(2pi i)^{-1} log u_1`,

\[
\Im\tau=-\frac{\log|t_c|}{2\pi}+\Im h_c(t_c)
\longrightarrow+\infty.
\tag{A5}
\]

## 4. The exact homogeneous sheaf

Use the two actual lifts in `SL_2(Z)`

\[
\widehat S_1=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\widehat S_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

They satisfy `S1hat^3=I`, `S2hat^4=I`, and `S1hat S2hat=[[1,1],[0,1]]`. Thus their lower-row factors give a well-defined cocycle `jtilde` with `jtilde(g_1,z)=-tau(z)`, `jtilde(g_2,z)=tau(z)`, and `jtilde(g_0,z)=1`. Directly, the finite products are

\[
(-1)^3\tau\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{-1}{\tau-1}=1,
\qquad
\tau\frac{-1}{\tau}\tau\frac{-1}{\tau}=1.
\]

Let `L` be the sheaf on `B` of homogeneous holomorphic functions `nu` on `pi^{-1}(U\{p_0})`, satisfying `nu(gz)=nu(z)/jtilde(g,z)` and extending at the distinguished cusp if `p_0∈U`.

The function `E_6 tau` has zeros of order two precisely over `p_2`. It has a global holomorphic square root `h_6` on `h_z`: on the complement of its zeros, analytic continuation of a square root around a meridian changes sign by `(-1)^2=1`; the same normal-generation argument as in §3 proves single-valuedness, and locally the square root extends with a simple zero. Put

\[
\Theta=\frac{(E_4\circ\tau)^2h_6}{\Delta_{\rm mod}\circ\tau}.
\tag{A6}
\]

For `j=1,2`, the quotient `h_6(g_jz)/(tau(z)^3 h_6(z))` has square one and removable singularities. It is a constant sign. At `z_1`, its value is `rho^{-3}=-1`. At `z_2`, the simple zero and `s_2(g_2z)=-i s_2(z)` give `h_6(g_2z)/h_6(z)→-i=i^3`, so the sign is `+1`. Applying the weight-four, weight-six, and weight-twelve identities consequently yields exactly

\[
\Theta(g_1z)=-\Theta(z)/\tau(z),\qquad
\Theta(g_2z)=\Theta(z)/\tau(z),\qquad
\Theta(g_0z)=\Theta(z).
\tag{A7}
\]

The orders are exactly two at `z_1`, one at `z_2`, and minus one in `t_c` at the distinguished cusp. At the cusp the invariance in (A7) makes `h_6` single-valued there; since its square is a holomorphic unit, it extends as a unit. Thus the assertion of a simple pole for `Theta` contains no hidden square-root monodromy.

Here is the full local sheaf map. At an ordinary point, `Theta` is nonzero, so `nu↦nu/Theta` is an isomorphism to `O_B`. At `z_1`, a nonzero homogeneous holomorphic germ `nu(s_1)=a s_1^k+...` satisfies

\[
\lambda_1^k=-1/\rho=\lambda_1^2.
\]

Therefore `k≡2 mod 3`, so `k≥2`, and `nu/Theta` is holomorphic. At `z_2` the identical leading-term calculation gives `lambda_2^k=1/i=-i=lambda_2`, hence `k≡1 mod 4` and `k≥1`. Again the quotient is holomorphic. In both cases that quotient is invariant, so its Taylor series contains only powers divisible by the stabilizer order; it descends holomorphically in `s_j^{m_j}`. Conversely multiplication of any descended holomorphic germ by `Theta` gives a homogeneous holomorphic germ. At the cusp, `Theta=t_c^{-1}v_c(t_c)` for a unit `v_c`, so the quotient `nu/Theta` extends and vanishes at `p_0` exactly when `nu` extends. These local maps agree on all overlaps because they are the same quotient by the global function `Theta`. Hence the exact global morphism is

\[
\mathcal L\xrightarrow{\ \nu\mapsto\nu/\Theta\ }
\mathcal O_B(-p_0),
\qquad
\psi\longmapsto(\psi\circ\pi)\Theta
\quad\hbox{for its inverse}.
\tag{A8}
\]

## 5. An explicit gluing proof of the two cohomology vanishings

This section proves the needed affine solvability without inferring any cusp regularity from Steinness. For the ideal line bundle `O(-p_0)`, use `e_0=Theta` over `U_0^B={|t|<2}` and `e_infinity=w Theta`, `w=1/t`, over `U_infinity^B={|w|<2}`. They are frames of the sheaf, including at the elliptic points by (A8). Their exact relation is

\[
e_\infty=t^{-1}e_0.
\]

An arbitrary locally nonempty affine torsor under this line bundle first has a smooth global section: take a finite smooth partition of unity subordinate to open sets with sections and form their affine weighted sum. The expression is independent of the affine origin because the weights add to one. On each of the two coordinate discs, this section can be made holomorphic by subtracting a local smooth solution of its `dbar` derivative. Such a solution exists directly from the Cauchy-Green operator: extend its smooth coefficient from a slightly smaller disc using a compactly supported cutoff in a slightly larger disc, then integrate against `1/(pi(z-zeta))`. The identity `dbar(1/(pi z))=delta_0` follows by Stokes' formula after deleting a circle about zero and taking its radius to zero. Thus the integral's `dbar` is the given coefficient on the smaller disc. Start with discs slightly larger than the two displayed ones, so this gives holomorphic torsor sections on both displayed sets.

Their difference on the annulus `1/2<|t|<2` is `H(t)e_0`. Its Laurent expansion is `H(t)=sum_(n∈Z) a_n t^n`, converging on that annulus. Define

\[
f_0(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nt^n,
\qquad
f_\infty(w)=-\sum_{n\le-1}a_nw^{-n-1}.
\]

The respective radii of convergence are at least two, by the positive/negative Laurent decomposition. On the overlap,

\[
H(t)=f_0(t)-t^{-1}f_\infty(1/t).
\]

Subtracting `f_0e_0` and `f_infinity e_infinity` from the two sections therefore glues them into one global holomorphic section. This proves every locally nonempty `O(-p_0)`-torsor trivial and, equivalently, `H^1(B,O(-p_0))=0`.

For a global section of `O(-p_0)`, the map (A8) gives a holomorphic function on compact `P^1` vanishing at `p_0`; the maximum principle makes it constant and its value at `p_0` makes it zero. Thus `H^0(B,O(-p_0))=0`.

For `O_B` the same proof uses equal frames on the overlap. Put `f_0(t)=sum_(n≥0)a_nt^n` and `f_infinity(w)=-sum_(n≤-1)a_n w^{-n}`. The equality is now `H=f_0-f_infinity`. It glues every locally nonempty additive torsor, proving `H^1(B,O_B)=0`. Global functions on `P^1` are constants by the same maximum principle, so `H^0(B,O_B)=C`. These are the precise two vanishing calculations used below.

## 6. Solving the affine mu problem with its cusp condition

The finite orbit tables obtained by actual iteration of (A1) are

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
&z&g_1z&g_1^2z\\ \hline
\tau&\tau&(\tau-1)/\tau&-1/(\tau-1)\\
\mu&\mu&(1-\mu)/\tau&(\tau-1+\mu)/(\tau-1)
\end{array}
\]

and

\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
&z&g_2z&g_2^2z&g_2^3z\\ \hline
\tau&\tau&-1/\tau&\tau&-1/\tau\\
\mu&\mu&1+\mu/\tau&1-\tau-\mu&(1-\mu)/\tau.
\end{array}
\]

The next application respectively returns `(tau,mu)`. As `Delta` is the free product of these cyclic groups, there are no other relations to check. Applying `g_2` and then `g_1` gives `(tau+1,mu)`, so the inverse word `g_0` gives `(tau-1,mu)` as prescribed.

Let `M` be the sheaf of affine solutions with the stated cusp extension. Differences are exactly sections of `L`, and addition of such a section preserves every condition. Local nonemptiness follows with no asymptotic assumption: set `mu=0` on an ordinary sheet and extend to its translates; at the cusp do so on the distinguished invariant component. At the elliptic discs take

\[
\mu_1=(2-\tau)/3,\qquad \mu_2=(1-\tau)/2.
\]

Their transforms are respectively `(tau+1)/(3tau)=(1-mu_1)/tau` and `(tau+1)/(2tau)=1+mu_2/tau`. Their stabilizers therefore preserve those local prescriptions; extension to all translated discs is well-defined. Thus `M` is a locally nonempty `L`-torsor. By (A8) and §5 it has one global section and no nonzero difference of two global sections. This proves existence and uniqueness of the actual cusp-regular `mu`.

Evaluation at the fixed points gives

\[
\mu(z_1)=\frac1{1+\rho}=\frac{2-\rho}{3},\qquad
\mu(z_2)=\frac{1-i}{2}.
\]

The identities `(1+rho)^2=3rho` and `(1-i)^2=-2i` then give exactly `6(1-mu(z_1))^2=2rho` and `6mu(z_2)^2=-3i`.

## 7. Solving the beta problem with its cusp condition

Fix the just-constructed `mu`, and put

\[
\phi_1=2-6(1-\mu)^2/\tau,\qquad
\phi_2=-3-6\mu^2/\tau.
\]

For the first cyclic orbit the sum is

\[
6-\frac6{\tau(\tau-1)}
\big((1-\mu)^2(\tau-1)+(\tau-1+\mu)^2-\mu^2\tau\big)=0,
\]

because the bracket expands to `tau(tau-1)`: its `mu^2` coefficient is `(tau-1)+1-tau=0`, its `mu` coefficient is `-2(tau-1)+2(tau-1)=0`, and its constant is `(tau-1)+(tau-1)^2=tau(tau-1)`.

For the second orbit the sum is

\[
-12+\frac6\tau
\big(-\mu^2+(\tau+\mu)^2-(1-\tau-\mu)^2+(1-\mu)^2\big)=0,
\]

because that bracket is `tau^2+2tau mu+2tau(1-mu)-tau^2=2tau`. The affine beta laws therefore close around both finite groups. Direct composition of `g_2` followed by `g_1` gives

\[
\beta(g_1g_2z)=\beta-3-\frac{6\mu^2}{\tau}
+2-\frac{6(-\mu/\tau)^2}{-1/\tau}=\beta-1.
\]

The inverse cusp word consequently gives `beta(g_0z)=beta+1`, so `b=beta+tau` is cusp-invariant.

At ordinary sheets set `beta=0`; at the distinguished cusp component set `beta=-tau`, giving `b=0`. At an elliptic disc with order `m_j`, take

\[
\beta_j=\frac1{m_j}\sum_{k=0}^{m_j-1}k\phi_j(g_j^kz).
\]

This is holomorphic and its difference under `g_j` is

\[
\frac1{m_j}\left(m_j\phi_j-\sum_{k=0}^{m_j-1}\phi_j(g_j^kz)\right)=\phi_j.
\]

Thus these are valid local solutions, which extend to translates because the cyclic sums vanish. Differences of local cusp-regular solutions are invariant holomorphic functions; at an elliptic point their invariant Taylor expansions descend in `s_j^{m_j}`, and at the cusp they are differences of the holomorphic functions `b`. The exact difference sheaf is therefore `O_B`. Section §5 glues these solutions globally. If `beta^part` is one resulting solution, all cusp-regular solutions and only those solutions are

\[
\beta=\beta^{\rm part}+c_0,\qquad c_0\in\mathbb C.
\tag{A9}
\]

## 8. Exact invariance, maximum, and scalar half-plane

Here is a direct verification of `D` invariance, independent of the period matrix. Write `tau=x+iy`, `mu=u+iv`, `Im beta=d`, and `Q=x^2+y^2`, with `y>0`. For any `a=u+iv` define

\[
K=vx-uy,\qquad H=2uvx-(u^2-v^2)y.
\]

Direct multiplication by the conjugate denominator gives

\[
\Im(a/\tau)=K/Q,\qquad
\Im(a^2/\tau)=H/Q,
\qquad yH+K^2=v^2Q.
\]

The last equality follows on expanding both terms: `2uvxy-u^2y^2+v^2y^2+v^2x^2-2uvxy+u^2y^2=v^2Q`. Under `g_2`, the new values are `y'=y/Q`, `v'=K/Q`, `d'=d-6H/Q`; the added `1` and `-3` in (A1) are real. Therefore

\[
D(g_2z)=d-\frac{6H}{Q}-\frac{6K^2}{Qy}
=d-\frac{6v^2}{y}=D(z).
\]

For `g_1` apply precisely the same calculation to `a=1-mu`, whose imaginary part is `-v`; its square is still `v^2`, and the beta increment's real constant is `+2`. For `g_0`, all changed entries in (A1) differ by real constants. Thus `D(gz)=D(z)` for every `g∈Delta`.

Because `mu,b,h_c` extend at the distinguished cusp, (A5) gives the exact formula

\[
D=\frac{\log|t_c|}{2\pi}
+\Im(b-h_c)
-\frac{6(\Im\mu)^2}{-\log|t_c|/(2\pi)+\Im h_c}.
\tag{A10}
\]

The middle term is bounded, the last term tends to zero, and its denominator stays positive. Consequently `D→-infinity`, uniformly in the angular cusp coordinate. Invariance transfers this same value to the quotient base; no boundedness of `mu` on other cusp components is used.

The function descends continuously to `B\{p_0}`, including `p_1,p_2`, since the original functions are holomorphic on their covering discs and `Im tau` has positive values there. Coarse smoothness at the orbifold points is unnecessary and is not asserted. To prove attainment of the maximum, choose any `b_*` in this base. By (A10), outside some compact set containing `b_*`, all values are smaller than `D(b_*)-1`. The continuous function attains a maximum on that compact set, which is therefore its maximum on the entire base. Define this actual maximum for `beta^part` by

\[
M=\max_{b\in B\setminus\{p_0\}}D_{\rm part}(b)\in\mathbb R.
\]

Equation (A9) gives `D=D_part+Im c_0`. Hence the exact parameter set is

\[
\boxed{D<0\ \hbox{everywhere}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Im c_0<-M.}
\tag{A11}
\]

At equality the maximum point has `D=0`. If `Im c_0>-M`, that point has positive `D`, whereas the cusp still has negative `D`. A path in the connected base from it toward the cusp therefore contains a zero by the intermediate-value theorem. Thus (A11) is also exactly the set of constants for which the period map is a real isomorphism everywhere; all other constants produce a real singularity somewhere. This strengthens the source's sufficient-negative-shift statement without changing its conclusion.
